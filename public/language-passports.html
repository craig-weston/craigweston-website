Language Passports.

What do you say when a bowl of noodles lands in front of you? The words are half the story. The other half is knowing why this is the moment to say them.

Play three practice stopsOpen the globe

A pocket-sized trip.

Visit the three stops in any order. Open a phrase card for a clue, then choose what you would do. You can try again as often as you like. Playing together? Take turns reading the scene and guessing the answer.

Three stamps to collect

Japan · Japanese

At the soba counter

You pull up a stool. The cook lifts a bundle of noodles out of the water, gives the strainer a shake and sets down your bowl. Your chopsticks are still on the counter.

Open the phrase card

いただきます · Itadakimasu

People often say this just before eating. It expresses gratitude for the meal and the work that went into it, from growing the ingredients to preparing the bowl.

Thailand · Thai

Hello at the market

Morning light catches the canal. A stallholder looks up from arranging mangoes. You want to ask about the fruit, but first there’s a moment to acknowledge the person behind the stall.

Open the phrase card

สวัสดี · Sawatdi

A greeting can open the conversation before you get down to business. You may see a wai, with the palms together. Its use varies with the relationship and setting.

China · Chinese

Finding the noodle shop

Somewhere down this lane in Xi’an is the noodle shop you’ve been looking for. You have its name written down. A passer-by pauses, and you need a way to begin your question.

Open the phrase card

请问 · Qǐngwèn

This Mandarin expression means “may I ask?” It gives the listener a moment to turn their attention to you. Then comes the actual question, or the name on your map.

Why I built it.

I think the reason behind a word deserves as much attention as the word itself. I could memorise a greeting on Monday and forget it by Friday. Give me a person to greet, somewhere to stand and a reason to speak, and I’ve got something to remember.

That’s what I wanted to put into a game. You can come for the writing, the food, the folklore or the pleasure of filling a passport with stamps. A child can spot a detail in a picture that an adult misses. Someone planning a trip can practise a phrase they’ll use next week. Both have a way in.
